The contribution

The expensive part
isn’t always the radio.

Towers, site work, specialist climbing, and permanent installations can dominate a rural network. Auren’s hypothesis is that reusable, mechanically locked structures and ground-serviceable equipment can remove enough of that work to change the economics.

A controller checks alternate paths before a relay changes height or comes down for maintenance. RF carries the access links. Optical transport may add capacity where conditions permit. All upstream internet is terrestrial.

Deployable masts, community meshes, and optical links already exist. Our proposed contribution is their mechanism and coordinated operation—not a claim that we invented wireless internet.

Geometry as infrastructure

Assemble low.
Work high.
Repair low.

The proposed mechanism separates the structure from the equipment it carries. Positive locks support the mast; an independent carriage makes radios replaceable at ground level.

First deployment

Open rural terrain, clustered homes, and two independent terrestrial internet handoffs. Start with qualified 2–5 km paths. Test the mast mechanism against a conventional installation.

What we do not claim

No forest penetration. No open-ocean or aviation equivalence. No guaranteed 100 Mbps to every home at once. No field-proven 99.9% availability. No established patentability.

The longer horizon

Stratospheric relays may extend reach without satellites. Year-round power, station-keeping, spectrum, capacity, and fleet economics are unresolved research—not features of this release.

Build, measure, decide

Three gates.
No hidden miracles.

  1. 30 days

    Prove height matters.

    Survey twenty homes with a rented certified mast. Get real upstream quotes. Build a low-height locking/lifting rig.

  2. 90 days

    Test the mechanism.

    Two experimental relays. Paid field work. Congestion, failures, battery endurance, and maintenance measured against a control network.

  3. One year

    Earn a deployment.

    Structural qualification, seasonal data, a 100–240-home pilot, and independently reviewed lifecycle costs.
